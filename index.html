Encode small, upscale to 4K

My bachelor thesis asked one question. If you shrink a 4K video to save bandwidth, can a neural network rebuild it better than plain bicubic scaling?

For the two small networks I tested, no. Bicubic stayed ahead on VMAF at every resolution and every compression level.

The ideas behind it

A short refresher on the technology, in the order the video travels through it.

Illustration of a player moving between versions

Why shrink a video at all

Raw 4K is far too heavy to send. Streaming services keep several versions of each video at different sizes and quality levels, called a bitrate ladder. The player moves up and down it as your connection changes.

  • 8.3 millionpixels in one 4K frame
  • About 6 Gbit/smy clip with no compression
  • 29.6 Mbit/sthe same clip as a normal H.264 file

My starting point was the DeepStream paper from the University of Klagenfurt. It sends devices with a GPU a small upscaling network along with the normal video, and reports bitrate savings of 34% at equal PSNR and 36% at equal VMAF for those devices.

Fewer pixels, less to send

Resolution is how many pixels a frame has. Going from 4K to 1080p keeps one pixel in four. Going to 270p keeps one in 64.

The upscaling step later has to fill in everything that was removed. The more you remove, the more it has to get right.

Store the changes, round off the detail

Neighbouring frames look almost the same. A codec stores a complete frame, then mostly records what moved. This is called inter-frame prediction.

It then rounds off fine detail. QP is the dial for how hard, from about 0 to 51. Low QP gives high quality and a big file. In my tests, each +5 QP cut the bitrate by more than half.

  • HEVCthe compression standard, also called H.265
  • x265the free encoder that implements it
  • FFmpegthe tool that ran every step

Each new pixel comes from its nearest neighbours

Bicubic: a smooth blend of neighbours

To create a new pixel, bicubic looks at the 4×4 block of known pixels around it and blends them along a smooth curve. The closest pixels count the most.

It knows nothing about the picture, so it cannot add real detail. It only smooths between what is already there.

  • Fastsimple maths on each pixel
  • No trainingthe same formula for every video
  • Slightly softthe baseline a network has to beat

Super-resolution: learned guessing

A super-resolution network trains on image pairs, a sharp original and a shrunken copy. It learns what detail usually goes with which pattern, then fills that in on new images.

FSRCNNWorks on the small image, then a final learned layer enlarges it.
ESPCNAlso works small, then rearranges its output channels into a larger image.

The catch is the training data. These 2016 models learned one clean kind of shrinking, and my video was also compressed. Newer models such as Real-ESRGAN and SwinIR train on messy, real-world damage.

PSNR, in dBCompares pixel by pixel. About 30 dB is mediocre, 40 dB and up is very good.
SSIM, 0 to 1Compares local structure, such as edges and texture. Closer to 1 is better.
VMAF, 0 to 100Netflix's score, trained on human opinion. 90 and up is excellent, under 50 is clearly degraded.

Three report cards for quality

Every upscaled video was compared against the original 4K clip, called the reference. Higher is better on all three scores.

The scores can disagree. PSNR and SSIM reward being exactly right, so a safe blur scores well and a plausible invented texture gets punished. This is the perception–distortion trade-off.

That is why I read VMAF first. At 1080p and QP 32, the networks trailed bicubic by about 6 dB on PSNR but only about 3 points on VMAF.

Starting resolution matters most

Pick the highest resolution your bitrate can fill. At 344 kbps, 720p reached VMAF 41.5. At 321 kbps, 1080p reached only 38.7, because it had too few bits for its pixels.

The networks stayed behind bicubic

The gap ran from under 1 VMAF point at the lowest bitrate to about 5–6 points at the highest, and up to about 9 points at 540p. PSNR makes the gap look larger than VMAF does.

Wrong training data

FSRCNN and ESPCN learned to undo clean bicubic downscaling. They received HEVC-compressed frames and partly sharpened the artefacts.

Bicubic has a head start

The small versions were made by roughly bicubic scaling, so bicubic upscaling recovers most of the low-frequency content.

Old, tiny models

Both date from 2016 and have only a few thousand parameters. Newer models are built for compressed video.

The first result was wrong

The first run showed bicubic ahead by about 9 dB, with network PSNR stuck near 33 dB across a 27× bitrate range. That looked too good to be true, so I checked the measurement itself.

A second lossy encode

OpenCV saved each network output with mp4v, a compression step the bicubic path never went through. Frames now go straight into the scorer.

Two colour conventions

OpenCV used BT.601 while the reference used BT.709. PSNR reacted strongly to this, and VMAF barely moved.

Fixes I tried and rejected
  • Convert network output to BT.709PSNR got worse, because it does not undo OpenCV's BT.601 decode.
  • Decode the reference through OpenCV tooVMAF fell to about 40 even for bicubic, because frame pairing broke.
  • Use cv2.resize for bicubicAbout 6 dB lower than FFmpeg bicubic, because its sampling grid is misaligned with the reference.

Pick the highest resolution your bitrate can fill

  • 1080pBest above about 0.5 Mbps.
  • 720pUseful only around 0.3–0.5 Mbps.
  • 540pNever the best choice, 720p beat it throughout.
  • 270pUnusable for 4K, with VMAF between 0 and 3.2.
